# How the research system works
*A plain-language explanation for the sceptical reader*

**The short version.** This is a system for producing deeply-researched articles on contested questions — and, more to the point, for making them *trustworthy*. It uses an AI model to do the legwork, but it is built on a blunt assumption about that model: left alone, it will state things confidently that aren't true, it won't remember what it did an hour ago, and it will tell you it's finished when it isn't. So the AI is never trusted to simply "go research this and report back." Research is treated as a process with checkpoints, and the AI is allowed past each one only when it can show its work. A person publishes. The machine never does.

**The core idea: claims, not answers.** Nothing here is "an answer." Every factual statement is a *claim*, and every claim has a status. It begins as a **lead** — something suspected, or seen in a single place — and it can only become a **finding**, the kind of thing that may be published, by passing a fixed set of checkpoints. That distinction is the whole game. A lead is "this might be true." A finding is "this survived an honest attempt to knock it down." Leads are never published as though they were findings — the difference between journalism and rumour — and that rule is enforced by the software, not by anyone's good intentions.

**Where the rules come from: harm first.** One rulebook does not fit all topics, so before any research begins, a rulebook for *this* topic is drafted — and the drafting starts from a blunt question: *who gets hurt if we publish something wrong here, and who gets hurt if we're right?* For health claims, the dominant harm is readers acting on bad information, so causal claims demand experimental evidence and anything a reader might act on gets a cooling-off period and a qualified second look. For an investigation that names people, defamation dominates, so every named-individual claim must anchor to a document you can point to. For a piece on garden layouts, almost nothing is at stake, and the rulebook thins out to the basics. Same machinery everywhere; the strictness is priced to the stakes.

**And no — the AI does not write its own rules.** The rulebook is *drafted* by an AI, because mapping a topic's risks and its real primary sources is exactly the kind of legwork it's good at. But it is proposed, never self-imposed: a separate adversarial review tries to break the draft, a human edits and freezes it, and only then does research begin under it. Beneath every topic rulebook sits a fixed universal floor that no rulebook can relax — every claim sourced, at least two independent sources, the opposing case written down, a human publishes. Topic rules can add to that floor; they can never subtract from it.

**The three gates a claim must pass.** Verification works in three layers, deliberately, so that cheap checks do most of the work and expensive judgement is spent only where it's needed. Together they enforce the topic's frozen rulebook, plus the universal floor.

1. **Automatic checks (free, run on every claim).** These are mechanical and need no intelligence: Does the claim cite a source at all? Are there at least *two independent* sources — not two outlets repeating one press release? Is at least one a **primary source**, the actual study or record rather than a report of it? Has someone written down the strongest *opposing* case? If a claim asserts that one thing *causes* another, is the evidence experimental rather than a mere correlation? A claim that fails any of these is bounced back automatically. Most weak claims die here, instantly, at no cost.

2. **The judge (a second AI, called at milestones).** A claim that clears the automatic checks is reviewed by a separate AI acting as a sceptical editor. Crucially, the judge is shown the claim and the rules but **not** the writer's reasoning — so it cannot be talked into agreement; it has to assess the thing on its own merits. It asks the questions a checklist can't: Are these two sources *really* independent? Does the confidence of the wording match the strength of the evidence? Was the opposing case answered, or just mentioned? The judge isn't run on every step — that would be slow and costly — but at the points that matter, and always when the writer claims a piece is finished.

3. **The advisor (the strongest model, summoned rarely).** When the judge hits something genuinely hard — a fine call it isn't sure about — it can escalate to a more powerful model for a single, narrow ruling. This is the expensive option, so it's used sparingly and its answers are cached. The advisor exists for exactly the cases where being approximately right isn't good enough.

Only when a claim clears the gates does a human — not the system — sign it off for publication.

**A worked example: "does extended screen time harm children?"** This is a genuinely hard question, which makes it a good test. The rulebook for this topic, drafted and frozen before work began, names the dominant harm plainly: parents changing how they raise their children on the strength of a wrong claim. So causal claims get the strictest evidence bar, and anything that reads as parenting advice is graded high-stakes. Now suppose the AI gathers studies and drafts the claim: *"Heavy screen use causes attention problems in children."*

- **The automatic checks** catch the first problem immediately. The claim says *causes*. Almost all the available evidence is **observational** — researchers looked at children who happen to use screens a lot and noticed more attention difficulties. But children who use screens heavily differ from those who don't in many other ways (sleep, home environment, income, what the screens replaced), and any of those could be the real driver. Observational data cannot establish *cause*. The check bounces the claim automatically: the wording outruns the evidence.

- The claim is **rewritten** to match what the data supports — *"Heavy screen use is associated with more attention problems, though the evidence does not establish that screens are the cause"* — and resubmitted with two independent sources, one a primary study.

- **The judge** now looks harder and spots a subtler issue: several of the cited studies measure screen time by *asking parents to estimate it*, which is unreliable, and the studies disagree on how large the association even is. It flags that the claim, as written, implies a firmer and tidier finding than the messy underlying literature supports.

- That disagreement is a fine judgement call — a real association, or an artefact of how it was measured? — so the judge **escalates to the advisor**. The advisor's narrow ruling: the association is real but modest, its size is genuinely contested, and the honest version must say so rather than imply a settled number.

- The claim that finally clears every gate is the carefully hedged one — association not causation, modest and contested in size, with the measurement caveat stated plainly. A person reads it, agrees, and signs. The confident headline the AI first drafted never makes it through.

**What it does not do.** It does not decide what is true. It decides what is *adequately supported to assert*, and a human makes the final call. It cannot launder an unsourced claim into a fact — there is no path through the software that allows it. Every published claim drags its evidence behind it: you can click any sentence and see the sources, the opposing case, and what the judge ruled. It keeps a running meter on its own cost with a hard stop, so it cannot quietly burn through money chasing a question. And when it gets something wrong — which it will — the correction is published in the open and stays on the record.

The point of the machinery is not speed. It is that the output earns the right to be believed, by showing, for every claim, exactly why it survived.
